# What a trial designer would do with this on day one

I would use this output to organize a short feasibility review. I would first
open the registry record and current protocol, confirm the extracted fields,
and check which signals were unassessable. A flag starts a question. It does
not establish a defect.

## Where each flag sends a human

| Flag | Where I would look | First question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrow age window | Population rationale, epidemiology, recruitment assumptions, and site patient counts | Is the age restriction essential to the scientific question or participant safety, and how many eligible patients will remain? |
| Long exclusion list | Full eligibility section, investigator guidance, and screen-failure assumptions | Which restrictions are essential, which duplicate another requirement, and what is their combined effect on eligibility? |
| Many outcomes | Endpoint hierarchy, statistical analysis plan, schedule of assessments, and participant materials | Are primary objectives clear, multiplicity addressed, and assessments feasible for participants and sites? |
| High enrollment per site | Recruitment period, site feasibility responses, competing studies, and historical accrual | Does the implied contribution per site fit the eligible patient pool and realistic recruitment time? |
| Large single-site trial | Staffing, referral pathways, equipment capacity, and contingency arrangements | What happens if this site's recruitment, staff availability, or assessment capacity falls short? |

For a missing or implausible value, I would verify the source before discussing
the score. A listed location is not necessarily an activated recruiting site.
Current enrollment may be actual enrollment after a study stopped, rather than
the original target.

I would also inspect a few low-scoring records. Missing information can produce
a low score, and important problems may fall outside these rules.

## The first review session

I would bring the highest-scoring records to a clinical and operational review,
with the flags visible beside the source values. For each flag, I would record
one of four outcomes: justified as written, source data incorrect, further
evidence needed, or a revision worth considering.

The useful action might be to confirm a safety restriction, obtain better
site-level estimates, simplify a redundant assessment, or document why an
apparently demanding design is appropriate. It would not automatically be to
remove criteria or reduce endpoints.

I would retain the input snapshot, rule version, reviewer rationale, and any
follow-up action. The score alone is insufficient documentation.

## Why transparent rules can be useful

In a regulated setting, a reviewer needs to trace an output to its inputs,
understand the rule, and explain a decision. This small rule set makes those
steps straightforward. It also makes errors and disagreements visible.

An opaque model might eventually provide useful additional information, but
a performance number would not explain whether a flag reflects clinically
meaningful design choices, incomplete data, or post-baseline changes.

Transparency does not make these thresholds valid or this software compliant
with any regulatory standard. It makes the assumptions easier to challenge,
document, and improve.

## What this must never be used for

This demo must not approve or reject a protocol, replace clinical or ethics
review, estimate an individual participant's risk, or recommend changing
safety criteria without clinical justification.

It must not label investigators as poor performers, rank sites for contracting,
deny funding, or claim that a study will terminate. It must not present current
registry data as a prospective prediction.

A low score must never be treated as evidence that a protocol is safe, feasible,
or ready to proceed. A high score must never be treated as evidence that the
design is inappropriate.

The day-one goal is a better documented human review, with clearer questions
and explicit uncertainty.
